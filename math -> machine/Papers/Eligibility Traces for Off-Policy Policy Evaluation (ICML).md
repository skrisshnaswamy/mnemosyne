## The Core Idea

You have a stream of experience produced by one policy. You want to know the value of a *different* policy. That is off-policy policy evaluation, and in 2000 almost nothing existed for it beyond one-step methods.

> [!NOTE] Behaviour policy vs target policy
> The **behaviour policy** $b$ is the one that actually chose the actions and generated the data. The **target policy** $\pi$ is the one whose value $Q^\pi(s,a)$ you want to estimate. Off-policy evaluation means $b \neq \pi$. ^behaviour-vs-target

The problem with multi-step methods is contamination. A one-step update uses one reward and one bootstrapped value; the action that produced that reward was chosen by $b$, but you can fix that at a single point. A multi-step update — the kind [[High-Dimensional Continuous Control Using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE)|eligibility traces]] give you — folds in rewards from a whole chain of actions, every one of them chosen by $b$. Plain TD($\lambda$) with $\lambda > 0$ just sums those up and never compensates. The estimate converges to the wrong thing.

> [!NOTE] Eligibility trace
> A per-state-action number $e_t(s,a)$ that says "how much credit should this pair get for the error I am seeing right now". It is set to 1 when the pair is visited and decays by $\gamma\lambda$ each step after. It turns one TD error into an update for the whole recent history at once, which is what makes TD($\lambda$) interpolate between one-step TD ($\lambda=0$) and Monte Carlo ($\lambda=1$). ^eligibility-trace

The insight of this paper is that the fix belongs *inside the trace*. Two different fixes, in fact, and they are the two halves of the paper:

1. **Per-decision importance sampling.** Multiply the trace by $\frac{\pi(s_t,a_t)}{b(s_t,a_t)}$ at every step, not just decay it by $\gamma\lambda$. The correction happens decision by decision as the trajectory unfolds, instead of one big product applied to a whole return at the end. Crucially, the reward at step $i$ gets weighted only by the ratios up to step $i$ — the future after $i$ should not affect how you weight a reward you already collected.

2. **Tree backup.** Do not correct at all. Instead, build a target that never needed correcting: at each step, back up the value of *every* action weighted by $\pi$, and only recurse into the one action the behaviour policy actually took. Since $b$'s choices only decide *where you look deeper*, not *how much weight anything gets*, $b$ vanishes from the algorithm entirely.

That second one is the real prize. Tree backup needs nothing from the behaviour policy except that it be **non-starving** — it never permanently stops visiting some state-action pair. $b$ can be non-stationary, non-Markov, and completely unknown. You do not need its action probabilities. Compare that to [[Doubly Robust Policy Evaluation and Learning|every importance-sampling estimator]], which is dead in the water without logged propensities. Tree backup is the ancestor of Retrace($\lambda$) and of the target used in modern off-policy actor-critics.

What it unlocks: learning about many policies in parallel from a single stream. That was the motivation — options and macro-actions (Sutton, Precup & Singh 1999) — but it is the same structure as offline evaluation of many ranking policies from one logged traffic stream.

## The Methodology

Episodic, tabular, model-free, offline updating (accumulate changes, apply at end of episode). $\gamma \in [0,1]$. Goal: estimate $Q^\pi(s,a)$ from episodes generated by $b$, where $b(s,a) > 0$ everywhere (soft) for the importance-sampling family.

**The classical baseline.** [[Counterfactual Risk Minimization|Importance sampling]] reweights samples drawn from the wrong distribution:

$$\mathbb{E}_d[x] = \mathbb{E}_{d'}\!\left[x\,\frac{d(x)}{d'(x)}\right]$$

Applied to whole episodes. Let $t_m$ be the first visit to $(s,a)$ in episode $m$, $R_m$ the return after it, and

$$w_m = \prod_{i=t_m+1}^{T_m-1} \frac{\pi(s_i,a_i)}{b(s_i,a_i)}$$

Then the ordinary (first-visit) estimator and its self-normalised cousin:

$$Q^{IS}(s,a) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} R_m w_m \qquad\qquad Q^{ISW}(s,a) = \frac{\sum_m R_m w_m}{\sum_m w_m}$$

$Q^{IS}$ is unbiased and consistent. $Q^{ISW}$ is **consistent but biased**, and usually far better behaved — when a rare trajectory produces a monstrous weight, that same weight sits in the denominator and damps the swing. This is exactly the self-normalised IPS trade you meet in [[Recommendations as Treatments- Debiasing Learning and Evaluation|off-policy estimation for recommenders]].

**Per-decision importance sampling.** Pull the weight inside the sum over rewards:

$$Q^{PD}(s,a) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{T_m-t_m} \gamma^{k-1} r_{t_m+k} \prod_{i=t_m+1}^{t_m+k-1}\frac{\pi_i}{b_i}$$

**Theorem 1**: $Q^{PD}$ is consistent and unbiased. The proof is short and worth internalising — split the full product into "before step $k$" and "from step $k$ onward", condition on the history, and note that the expected value of the trailing product $\mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{i \ge t+k} \pi_i/b_i \mid s_{t+k}, a_{t+k}, b\right] = 1$. The future weights are, in expectation, dead weight. Dropping them changes nothing in the mean and strictly cuts variance.

The weighted version $Q^{PDW}$ divides by the sum of the per-step weights $\sum_m\sum_k \gamma^{k-1}\prod \pi_i/b_i$. Consistent, biased.

**Algorithm 1 — per-decision with traces.** The whole change to TD($\lambda$) is one extra factor in the trace decay:

$$e_t(s,a) = e_{t-1}(s,a)\,\gamma\lambda\,\frac{\pi(s_t,a_t)}{b(s_t,a_t)}, \qquad e_t(s,a)=1 \text{ at first visit}$$
$$\delta_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma\,\frac{\pi(s_{t+1},a_{t+1})}{b(s_{t+1},a_{t+1})}\,Q_t(s_{t+1},a_{t+1}) - Q_t(s_t,a_t)$$
$$Q_{t+1}(s,a) \leftarrow Q_t(s,a) + \alpha\, e_t(s,a)\,\delta_t$$

Offline, with $\lambda = 1$ and $\alpha(s,a) = 1/n(s,a)$, this *exactly* reproduces $Q^{PD}$; another choice of $\alpha$ reproduces $Q^{PDW}$. **Theorem 2**: for soft stationary $b$ and any $\lambda \in [0,1]$ not depending on $a_t$, the offline version converges w.p.1 to $Q^\pi$ under the usual [[Adam- A Method for Stochastic Optimization|step-size]] conditions (Robbins–Monro). Proof via Jaakkola–Jordan–Singh (1994): show the corrected $n$-step return is a $\gamma^n$-contraction in the max norm, then note any convex mixture of $n$-step returns inherits the fixed point.

**Tree backup.** The $n$-step target:

$$Q^{TB}_n(s,a) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_m \left[ \gamma^n Q(s_{t_m+n},a_{t_m+n})\!\!\prod_{i=t_m+1}^{t_m+n}\!\!\pi_i \;+\!\! \sum_{k=t_m+1}^{t_m+n}\!\! \gamma^{k-t_m+1}\!\!\prod_{i=t_m+1}^{k-1}\!\!\pi_i\left(r_k + \gamma\!\!\sum_{a \neq a_k}\!\!\pi(s_k,a)Q(s_k,a)\right)\right]$$

Read it off the backup diagram: at every node you fan out over all actions. The ones you did *not* take contribute their current estimate $Q(s_k,a)$, weighted by $\pi(s_k,a)$, and stop there. The one you *did* take is expanded one level deeper. Only $\pi$ appears. At $n=1$ this collapses to plain TD(0).

**Algorithm 2 — tree backup with traces.**

$$e_t(s,a) = e_{t-1}(s,a)\,\gamma\lambda\,\pi(s_t,a_t)$$
$$\delta_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma\sum_{a\in A}\pi(s_{t+1},a)\,Q(s_{t+1},a) - Q(s_t,a_t)$$

Same update rule. Note the trace decays by the target probability $\pi(s_t,a_t) \le 1$, so traces die fast whenever the behaviour policy takes an action the target thinks unlikely — this is the "cut the trace on an exploratory action" behaviour, and in the control case both algorithms reduce to Watkins's Q($\lambda$).

That fast decay is a practical problem, and the paper's fix is a hyperparameter that matters more than it looks: **divide the trace by $\max_a \pi(s_t,a)$ at each step.** Without it the traces collapse to near one-step behaviour. $\lambda$ may depend on the state but never on the action.

**Theorem 3**: for any non-starving $b$ (non-stationary, non-Markov, unknown all fine), and any action-independent $\lambda$, offline Algorithm 2 converges w.p.1 to $Q^\pi$. Proof by induction on $n$ showing $\gamma$-contraction, then Jaakkola et al. again.

## Ablation Studies and Experiments

**Setup.** 100 randomly generated [[Markov Decision Process|MDPs]]. Each: 100 non-terminal states + 1 terminal, 2 actions per state, each action branching to 4 randomly chosen successors with random probabilities (partition of unity from 3 uniform split points). Rewards uniform on $[0,1]$. $\gamma = 1$. Fixed start state. Target policy: action 1 with 80%, action 2 with 20%.

Two behaviour policies:
- **Uniform**: 50/50.
- **Different**: 20/80 — the exact opposite of the target, the hard case.

All six estimators run offline, which makes them parameter-free (no $\alpha$ or $\lambda$ to tune), precisely so the comparison is about the underlying estimator and not about tuning. Metric: mean squared error against the true action values, averaged over 100 MDPs × 200 state-action pairs, over 1000 episodes. Unvisited pairs excluded.

**Results, ranked.**

| | Uniform behaviour | Different behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Tree backup | best in medium/long run | best in medium/long run |
| Weighted IS | slightly better very early | 2nd |
| Ordinary IS | slow, high variance | slow, high variance |
| Per-decision IS | efficient long-run | relatively slow |
| Weighted per-decision IS | **poor** | poor, but beats plain PD |
| One-step TD(0) | mid | mid |

Final MSE scale: uniform panel tops out around 150 and the spread is wide at episode 1000; the "different behaviour" panel tops out around 180, and every method degrades when $b$ and $\pi$ disagree.

**Tree backup wins essentially everywhere**, losing only to weighted IS by a small margin at very small episode counts. It also has the weakest assumptions. The authors' summary is unambiguous: tree backup, on both performance and requirements.

**What did not work.**

- **Ordinary importance sampling is the clear loser.** Unbiased and consistent, and still practically bad — the product of ratios over a long episode has enormous variance. Unbiasedness is not the property you want.
- **Weighted per-decision IS is the surprise failure.** Every prior in the paper says self-normalising should help: it helped a lot for the Monte Carlo estimator ($Q^{ISW} \gg Q^{IS}$). Applied to the per-decision estimator it performed "fairly poorly in both cases". The authors flag it as surprising and do not explain it. The two variance reductions — per-decision truncation and self-normalisation — do not compose.
- **Plain TD($\lambda$), $\lambda > 0$**, is not even in the running. It is not a wrong-performing baseline, it is an incorrect algorithm here: the multi-step transitions are contaminated by $b$ and nothing compensates.
- **Untamed tree-backup traces.** Without the $\max_a \pi(s_t,a)$ normalisation the traces "decay too quickly" — the multi-step advantage evaporates.

**The proposed hybrid was never tested.** Section 6 observes that the two methods can be mixed per state — use per-decision IS where $b$ is known and Markov, tree backup where it is not — without losing convergence. "We have not yet explored this idea empirically."

## Worth Remembering

**Limitations the authors state plainly.** Tabular, model-free, offline updating only. No function approximation. Prediction only, not control (though both reduce to Watkins's Q($\lambda$) in the control case with a deterministic target). Several of the algorithms *could* be applied online; the experiments deliberately are not, so all six are compared on equal footing.

**The conceptual picture (Figure 4) is the thing to keep.** Draw the full trajectory tree from $(s,a)$: hollow circles are states, filled circles are actions. Per-decision IS walks one path down the tree and reweights the rewards it collected along that path. Tree backup takes a horizontal cut across the tree, using estimates for all the branches it did not follow. That is *why* $b$ disappears from tree backup — the behaviour policy only decides which branch to expand, never how much anything counts.

**The variance lesson generalises well beyond RL.** Three separate variance reductions appear here: (i) drop future importance ratios that have expectation 1 — free, no bias; (ii) self-normalise — costs bias, usually pays; (iii) replace sampling with exact expectation wherever you can enumerate — tree backup's whole trick, and the same move [[Doubly Robust Policy Evaluation and Learning|DR estimators]] make with the direct method term. (iii) beat (i) and (ii) here.

**The propensity-free property is the practical headline.** If your logging system did not record action probabilities, or the logging policy was a heuristic rules engine rather than a stochastic model, IPS-family estimators are unusable. Tree backup only needs $\pi$ — which you own — and coverage of the action space. That is a genuinely different operating regime from [[A Contextual-Bandit Approach to Personalized News Article Recommendation (LinUCB)|the replay evaluator]] or [[Open Bandit Dataset and Pipeline- Towards Realistic and Reproducible Off-Policy Evaluation|logged-propensity OPE pipelines]].

**Caveat on that headline.** Tree backup pays for it with bias from bootstrapping (it leans on current $Q$ estimates for unexplored branches) and with traces that shrink by $\pi(s_t,a_t) \le 1$ every step. The further $b$ drifts from $\pi$, the shorter the effective horizon — it quietly degrades towards one-step TD instead of exploding in variance. That is a much nicer failure mode than IPS's, but it *is* a failure mode. Retrace($\lambda$) (Munos et al. 2016) later addressed exactly this by using $\min(1, \pi/b)$ traces, sitting between per-decision IS and tree backup.

**Open questions.** Why does self-normalisation break per-decision IS? Does the per-state hybrid of Section 6 actually help? And the obvious modern one: none of the convergence proofs survive function approximation — with a neural $Q$, off-policy + bootstrapping + approximation is the deadly triad, and this paper's guarantees do not apply.
